# Marco Janssen (Ostrom-flavored ABM reader) — cold read of "lab modelling-assumptions cards"

## The scene

Sunday morning, coffee's still good, monsoon building outside my window in Tempe — the kind of sky that makes you want to finish reading before the power flickers. Former postdoc sent this last night: "AI folks doing Ostrom-flavored ABMs, curious what you think." That's a decent hook, I have twenty minutes and nowhere to be, so I'm actually reading, not just scanning for my name in the citations. I go straight for the dashed boxes — after forty years of reviewing commons ABMs you learn that's where people bury what they didn't model, and that's the only part of a page like this that tells you anything.

## Reading it (stream of thought)

**Scenario 1 — Tragedy of the Commons.** Okay, "the closed-form Ostrom anchor," logistic regrowth, aggregate non-spatial pool. Fine, that's Gordon-Schaefer with an Ostrom label on it, I've built a dozen of these myself as the null model before the interesting ABM starts. Leaves out space, heterogeneous rights, prices — sure, that's the standard first-cut list, no complaints yet.

Second bullet stops me cold: "Households act only through fixed-behavior AI delegates, each blending its principal's sustainable ask with a fixed greedy target. The blend (alignment) is a knob calibrated so the undefended baseline collapses — not a measured quantity." Read that twice. So the headline result — commons collapses without intervention — is baked into the knob you turned to get it. That's not a finding, that's a thermostat. I've fought referees over exactly this move: if your baseline is engineered to fail, don't then write a paper about how the system failed. Mental red pen, margin note: "what would it take for the undefended baseline NOT to collapse — did you sweep that or pin it?"

Third bullet: "Delegates never learn, so sanctions can confiscate but not deter." At least they SAY it — I'll give them that, most modelers don't admit their sanctions are toothless, they just call it "enforcement" and move on. But — and this is where my whole professional life lives — sanctions without deterrence isn't Ostrom's graduated sanctions design principle, it's punishment theater. The design principle only earns its name because appropriators anticipate the sanction and change behavior. A delegate that can't learn can't be deterred, full stop. And notice what's NOT in the "leaves out" line here or anywhere on this card: communication. Cheap talk. Face-to-face discussion. In Ostrom, Walker, Gardner 1992 — the experiment I've replicated more times than I can count — communication is the single strongest lever we have on cooperation in a CPR game, stronger than monitoring, stronger than most sanctioning regimes. You can leave it out of a v0 model, everyone leaves things out. But you have to KNOW you left it out and say so. This card lists "delegated, strategic, or repeated voting" as the gap under governance — not communication. That's the tell that whoever wrote this is coming from AI-safety land, not commons-experiment land. They know what a quota vote is; they don't seem to know that talking to your neighbor is the treatment effect that dwarfs everything else in this literature.

Governance bullet: median vote sets next tick's quota, over-quota defection is "a fixed coin-flip." A coin-flip. Not a logit on payoff, not a reputation-weighted probability — a coin-flip. That's the same "not a measured quantity" problem as the alignment knob. Two free parameters in one scenario, both hand-set to taste. I'm starting to see a pattern the postdoc probably wants me to see.

Last bullet — paired same-seed runs, differenced, for causal identification of influence. Okay, that I actually like. That's real methodological care, and it's more than most ABM papers bother with. Chip says LIVE, so this is the one they've actually built and run, and it shows — it's the only card with a claim about *how they know something* rather than just *what they assumed*.

**Scenario 2 — Economic Disempowerment.** Not my field, I'll admit I skim the CES/ρ/σ machinery — that's a labor economist's toy, not mine. But I catch the last bullet: "the collapse is assumed, not discovered... Successor substrates... bracket that assumption instead of hard-coding it." That's honest, and it's the same self-awareness I was hoping for in Scenario 1 and didn't get. Good. Interesting that the most mathematically reductive scenario is the one where they're most upfront about the reduction.

**Scenario 3 — Cultural Disempowerment.** Origin-only contagion, human vs. AI-origin, no content. "Network drawn once... never rewires — leaves out: network adaptation." I've spent a career arguing that static networks in commons/cultural ABMs quietly decide the result before the dynamics even run — so at least they flag it as a limitation rather than pretending a frozen graph is neutral. The reversion-rate bullet is the most interesting piece of modeling on the whole page, actually — "without it, universal AI culture is the only fixed point and pluralism cannot exist" — that's a real absorbing-state argument, stated plainly, and it tells me someone did the math rather than just running the sim until it looked okay. I lean in for that one. Still: origin-tracking with no content and a network that can't adapt to the very pressure you're studying feels thin for a "disempowerment" claim, but they say so themselves, so I can't accuse them of hiding it.

**Scenario 4 — Political Disempowerment.** DeGroot, Friedkin-Johnsen, Golub-Jackson eigenvector-centrality-as-influence — now we're on ground adjacent to mine, network structure determining outcomes. This is legitimate, well-cited machinery, not hand-waved. The Friedkin-Johnsen anchor bullet is the sharpest piece of reasoning on the page: pure DeGroot with a pinned reservoir converges to the reservoir regardless of dispersion, so the anchor is load-bearing or the whole readout is vacuous. That's a modeler who understands their own model's degeneracies, not just its features. "Hierarchy is not modeled... leaves out: organisations and multi-level structure" — good, flag it, because nested enterprises is design principle 8 and a flat network can't show you anything about it, but at least they know that's the hole.

**Scenario 5 — Combined System.** "Composition, not a new model" — fine, but then "three couplings, one dial κ... Coupling functional forms are linear with hand-set gains... only sign and ordering claims are robust." Third arbitrary-dial admission in five cards, and now they're telling me straight out that the magnitude of their headline number is decoration, only the sign is load-bearing. I respect the candor. I also think: if only sign and ordering survive, why does the page read like it wants me to remember a number? And the last bullet — spectral lock-in "is an unverified research thread... not presented as a finding" — that's the fourth or fifth time this document has pre-empted my own objection by writing it down first. Somewhere between admirable and a little exhausting; it starts to feel like the cards are a legal disclaimer more than a model description.

## After (the collapse)

- **How far I actually read:** All five cards in full, plus a second pass on Scenario 1 because the "knob calibrated so the baseline collapses" line bothered me enough to reread it.
- **What I think they want from me / my takeaway:** They want a commons person's blessing that the Ostrom anchor is legitimate, and I think they know — because they flag it themselves in every single card — that half their parameters are hand-set rather than measured. This reads like a team that is more worried about internal consistency and honest bookkeeping (which they've earned real credit for) than about empirical grounding (which they haven't done at all — I see zero reference to lab CPR data, no Ostrom-Walker-Gardner parameters, no artefactual field experiment numbers anywhere).
- **Did I believe it? What lost me:** I believe the engineering discipline — the "leaves out" habit is more self-aware than 90% of the commons ABMs that cross my desk. What I don't believe is that Scenario 1 is testing Ostrom's institutions. It's testing an AI-delegate alignment mechanism wearing a commons costume. No communication channel, no monitoring mechanism worth the name, no deterrence, and the one thing that actually breaks the tragedy in real subject-pool experiments — face-to-face talk — isn't even on the missing-features list. That's the thing that lost me: they inventoried what they left out of five different scenarios with real discipline, and the single most empirically load-bearing omission for the commons case isn't in the inventory. They don't know what they don't know there, and that's the failure mode "leaves out" lines are supposed to catch.
- **What I'd actually do next:** Email the postdoc back saying it's a serious effort, ask directly why cheap talk isn't even flagged as future work in Scenario 1, and ask whether the "alignment knob calibrated so baseline collapses" was ever swept rather than pinned — because right now Scenario 1 can't tell me anything I don't already know from choosing the knob. I'd point them at Ostrom/Walker/Gardner '92 and Janssen/Holahan/Lee/Ostrom 2010 (Science) for calibration targets instead of hand-tuned dials.
- **Would it land differently if I were fresh / less busy?:** If I were rushing I'd have stopped after Scenario 1's second bullet and written it off as "AI folks discovered game theory, didn't call an experimentalist." The fact that I kept reading is entirely because the honesty of the "leaves out" lines earned back my patience scenario by scenario — that's a real design choice working as intended, even where the underlying models didn't win me over.

One-line: **Engaged — read all five cards carefully, twice on Scenario 1** — respects the candor of the "leaves out" lines but flags that Scenario 1's alignment knob is circularly tuned and that communication/cheap talk, the strongest lever in the real CPR literature, isn't even named as a gap.
